To bake a good loaf of bread you need four things: flour, water, salt, and time. The flour supplies the starch and the proteins that become gluten when it is wetted and worked. The water wakes the flour and lets the yeast move. The salt tightens the dough and slows the yeast just enough that the flavor has room to develop. Time does the rest. A baker mixes the dough until it is shaggy, rests it for half an hour, and then folds it gently every so often instead of kneading it hard. Each fold stretches the gluten a little more and traps small bubbles of gas from the yeast. After several hours the dough has doubled and feels light, like a sleeping animal. It is shaped, laid in a floured basket, and left to rise once more, either on the counter for an hour or in the cold for a whole night. The oven must be very hot, and a pan of steam keeps the crust soft long enough for the loaf to spring upward before it hardens, turns brown, and begins to crackle as it cools.
