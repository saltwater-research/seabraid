A harbor town keeps its records in a stone building near the water. Every morning the clerk opens the shutters, lights the stove, and sets out the ledgers on a long oak table. The ledgers list every ship that has entered the bay for two hundred years: the name of the vessel, the port it came from, the cargo it carried, and the tide on which it arrived. Some pages are stained with salt, and some are written in hands so faded that only the clerk can read them. When a captain asks about an old voyage, the clerk does not search at random. He knows that grain ships came in spring, that timber came after the first frost, and that the spice traders always arrived on the highest tides of summer. He runs a finger down the columns, murmuring dates, until a single line stops him. Then he copies it onto a clean sheet, blots the ink, and hands it across the table with a small nod, as though returning something that had only been borrowed from the sea. In the evening he closes the shutters again, banks the stove, and walks home along the quay, listening to the rigging tap against the masts.
